## I. Platonic Tradition

### Primary Texts

**Phaedo (c. 360 BCE)**
Socrates' final dialogue, delivered on the day of his death. Contains the most sustained argument for the soul's immortality and pre-existence. The "Argument from Recollection" (72e–77a) is the philosophical core: if we know things (like mathematical equality) that can never be perfectly instantiated in the sensory world, we must have known them before birth. The soul saw the Forms before incarnation.

**Meno (c. 380 BCE)**
Contains the famous demonstration: Socrates guides an uneducated slave boy to solve a geometry problem using only questions. The boy "discovers" truths he was never taught. Plato's conclusion: the knowledge was always already present; the questioning merely recovered it. This is the empirical demonstration of anamnesis.

**Phaedrus (c. 370 BCE)**
Describes the soul's pre-natal journey in mythological terms: souls travel in a great procession behind the gods, glimpsing the Forms from the heavens. Embodiment interrupts this vision. Those who glimpse the Forms most fully before birth will be born with the most philosophical natures.

**Republic, Book X (c. 375 BCE)**
The Myth of Er: a soldier killed in battle is allowed to observe the between-life process. Souls choose their next lives from a range of available destinies — but they choose with the character they cultivated in their previous lives. This introduces a recursive structure: prior lives shape the choosing capacity for future lives.

### Scholarly Context
- A. E. Taylor, *Plato: The Man and His Work* (1926) — classic treatment of the immortality arguments
- Dominic Scott, *Recollection and Experience* (1995) — modern rehabilitation of anamnesis against empiricist objections
- Gail Fine, "Inquiry in the Meno" — analysis of whether the slave boy case actually proves recollection

## II. Yoruba Ori — Extended Treatment

### Cosmological Framework

The Yoruba worldview (particularly as systematised by scholars including Wande Abimbola and Kola Abimbola) distinguishes multiple components of personhood:

- **Emi** — the breath of life; the animating principle given by Olodumare (the Supreme Being)
- **Ori** — the inner head; the personal divinity; the carrier of destiny
- **Ara** — the body; the physical container
- **Ojiji** — the shadow; associated with the ancestral self

Of these, the *ori* is the most philosophically significant. It is sometimes described as more important than the orishas themselves — because the orishas can intercede, but the ori is the most direct expression of one's divine appointment.

### The Pre-Natal Moment

The moment of ori-selection is understood in Ifa divination literature (the *Odu*) as occurring in *Ile-Ori* — sometimes described as the atelier or workshop of Ajala, the divine sculptor of heads. Souls present themselves and choose (or are assigned) an ori from among those Ajala has fashioned. The quality of the choice — and the quality of Ajala's craftsmanship — determines the general arc of the earthly life.

**Critical nuance:** Not all ori are equally well-constituted. A poorly chosen or poorly crafted ori produces a person whose destiny is difficult. This is not moral failure — it is pre-natal circumstance. The role of Ifa divination is partly to identify one's ori and align one's earthly conduct with its best expression.

### Epistemological Significance

The ori is the Bayesian prior in its most complete form:
- It is **pre-loaded** before any experience
- It is **structured** (not a blank slate)
- It has **degrees of calibration** (good or poor ori)
- It is **revisable through practice** (alignment rituals, Ifa consultation, moral conduct) — but not arbitrarily revisable
- It **persists through death** and may be carried into future iterations

- Segun Gbadegesin, *African Philosophy: Traditional Yoruba Philosophy and Contemporary African Realities* (1991)

## III. Vedantic Atman — Extended Treatment

### Upanishadic Foundation (c. 800–200 BCE)

The *Atman* (self) is described across the Upanishads as identical in ultimate nature with *Brahman* (the absolute ground of being). The *Chandogya Upanishad*'s "Tat tvam asi" (That art thou) is the compressed formula: the individual self is not separate from the universal self. This makes the Vedantic position not merely preexistentist but **eternalist** — the self was never created and will never be destroyed.

**Brihadaranyaka Upanishad:** Contains the key argument that the self that perceives cannot itself be perceived — suggesting its irreducibility to any empirical category. The self is the witness, never the witnessed.

### The Bhagavad Gita (c. 200 BCE–200 CE)

Krishna's declaration to Arjuna (2.19–2.20):
- The self was never born; it will never die
- It does not come into being when the body begins; it does not cease when the body ends
- It is unslain when the body is slain

**Philosophical implication:** The self that enters each life already carries the accumulated samskaras of all prior lives. This is not merely metaphysical — it explains why some individuals seem naturally disposed to certain traits, capacities, or wisdom at birth.

### Samskaras as Running Prior

The *samskara* concept is philosophically rich:
- Samskaras are impressions formed by actions, thoughts, and intentions across lives
- They accumulate, reinforce, or counteract one another
- They constitute the "prior probability distribution" that the soul carries into each new incarnation
- Through disciplined practice (yoga, meditation, ethical conduct), one can reshape the samskara profile — Bayesian updating at the level of the soul

### Liberation as Prior Dissolution

*Moksha* is not the acquisition of a new state — it is the recognition of what always already was: the Atman's unconditioned nature, prior to and independent of all samskaras. Liberation is the self realising its own preexistence with radical clarity. The ultimate prior is pure awareness itself — beyond conditioning, beyond update.

### Primary Sources
- Sarvepalli Radhakrishnan, *The Principal Upanishads* (1953)
- Eliot Deutsch, *Advaita Vedanta: A Philosophical Reconstruction* (1969)
- Karl Potter (ed.), *Encyclopedia of Indian Philosophies* — Advaita Vedanta volume

## IV. Origen of Alexandria — Extended Treatment

### Historical Context

Origen (c. 185–254 CE) was the most prolific and philosophically sophisticated theologian of early Christianity. Educated in the Platonic tradition and deeply engaged with Gnostic thought, he attempted to synthesise Christian theology with Greek philosophy.

### De Principiis — Core Arguments

**The Pre-cosmic Fall:**
God created all rational souls (*logikoi*) equal, in a state of pure contemplative union with the divine. These souls possessed free will — and some used it to turn away from God, falling into greater or lesser degrees of alienation. The material world was created by God as a rehabilitative environment, calibrated to each soul's degree of fall.

**Theodicy and Pre-natal Moral History:**
This framework provides Origen's solution to theodicy: the apparent inequalities of earthly life — some born wealthy, some into poverty; some with natural gifts, some with severe limitations — are not divine injustice. They are the consequences of pre-natal moral choices. Every earthly condition is, in a precise sense, *deserved* — not in a punitive sense, but in a corrective, pedagogical one.

**Universal Restoration (Apokatastasis):**
Origen proposed that all souls — including, controversially, the Devil — would eventually return to full union with God. The process of incarnation and experience is ultimately educational: the material world is God's school, and every soul will eventually graduate.

### Condemnation and Significance

The Second Council of Constantinople (553 CE) condemned Origenism, citing:
- Pre-existence of souls
- The spherical bodies of the resurrected
- Universal salvation (apokatastasis)

Despite condemnation, Origen's ideas influenced significant strands of Christian mysticism, including Evagrius Ponticus and, indirectly, much of the contemplative tradition.

### Primary Sources
- Origen, *De Principiis* (translated by G. W. Butterworth, 1936)
- John Anthony McGuckin, *The Westminster Handbook to Origen* (2004)
- Henri Crouzel, *Origen* (1989)

## V. Kantian A Priori — Bridge Analysis

### The Copernican Revolution in Epistemology

Kant's critical turn (1781) reversed the traditional assumption that the mind conforms to objects. Instead, Kant argued: *objects conform to the mind*. The mind brings its own structure to all possible experience — structure that cannot be derived from experience because it is the condition for the possibility of experience.

**The A Priori Forms:**
- **Sensibility:** Space and time (the *a priori* forms of intuition)
- **Understanding:** The twelve categories (causality, substance, unity, plurality, necessity, etc.)
- **Reason:** The ideas of Soul, World, and God (constitutive for thought, not empirically accessible)

### The Preexistentialist Reading

Kant explicitly rejected soul preexistence. But his account of the mind's structure is a philosophical secularisation of the same claim:
- The mind comes to experience *already structured*
- That structure is not learned; it is constitutive
- Without it, experience would be "blind" — a mere sensory flood, not knowledge

**The gap Kant leaves:** He explains the structure of the transcendental subject (the "I think" that accompanies all representations), but he cannot explain *why* this particular subject has this particular character, disposition, or orientation. The traditions above — particularly the Yoruba ori — offer what Kant's system lacks: an account of the *individual prior*, not merely the universal cognitive structure.
